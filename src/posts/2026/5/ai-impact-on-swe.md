I am generally not a fan of conferences. Most feel more like product showcases than actual learning. The AI Dev 26 x SF conference organized by DeepLearning.ai was different. It gave me a lot to think about, particularly around where software engineers fit in a world increasingly shaped by AI. Getting to meet Andrew Ng, who first introduced me to deep learning close to ten years ago, made it even more memorable.


<hr>

One of the key ideas from the conference was the *K-shaped economy*. As AI models and agents improve, the lower-level tasks in software engineering become easier to automate. Roles that focus on implementation work - writing code for a specific framework, following established patterns - are increasingly within reach of agents. At the same time, roles that require higher-order thinking, things like system design, problem framing, and stakeholder alignment, are becoming more valuable.

The middle ground is shrinking. That is what the K-shape describes. The lower end gets absorbed by agents. The upper end gets amplified by them.

*Development speed is no longer the bottleneck*. There are agents that specialise in coding, security, databases, and more. Teams can now fill gaps temporarily using agents rather than waiting for headcount. The bottleneck has shifted elsewhere - to governance, compliance, and stakeholder buy-in, areas that still require human judgment.

One data point that stuck with me: the ratio of engineers to product managers has been compressing. What once required many engineers per PM may now trend toward one-to-one. In some cases, *a single person might cover both sides*. That is not universal - regulations and organisational structure will limit how far this goes - but the direction is clear.

It raises a question worth sitting with: could we be heading toward a dark factory model, where entire systems are built and maintained by agents with minimal human involvement?

<hr>

That question brings me to where engineers like me should be heading.

The answer is not to panic, but to *adapt deliberately*. Being generalist enough to handle cross-functional responsibilities - basic stakeholder communication, thinking across the stack - will matter more. Not because engineers should become product managers, but because the gap between those roles is narrowing.

Deep expertise still matters too. Not necessarily the hands-on implementation, but the technical knowledge and judgment to supervise agents, catch mistakes, and reason about real-world constraints. That combination - broad enough to collaborate, deep enough to verify - is hard to replace.

The pace of change also makes it tempting to chase every new technology. I think that is a trap. A solid grasp of fundamentals will take you further than scrambling after each new release.

What surprised me most was the *emphasis on writing*. Engineers are trained to think logically, but translating that into clear, precise language is its own skill. In a world where much of the work involves instructing agents, writing becomes a core part of the job. Not polished prose - clear thinking, written down.

<hr>

Whatever direction this goes, it is both exciting and uncertain. I may be doing a very different kind of work in a few years. That is not necessarily a bad thing, but it does mean being more intentional now about what I choose to develop in myself.
