At the start of my software engineering journey, I didn't know how to do most of the things the job required. I didn't know basic Linux commands like `grep`, and I couldn't write SQL to query my own database. I felt like I was in the wrong field, unable to do the most basic tasks. It was straightforward impostor syndrome.

That gap drove me to spend a lot of time learning: basic Linux commands, using git to version control my work, writing SQL to query and modify tables. I also worked to get certified in AWS Solutions Architect, which forced me through computer science fundamentals I knew nothing about at the start - networking, security etc.

Three years in, there's still plenty to learn, but I'm in a different place than where I started. I can write SQL to manipulate data quickly, work across multiple git branches without thinking twice, deploy apps to Kubernetes etc.

<hr>

Despite that, I don't think I've fully mastered the basics - Linux, git, Docker are all still shallow for me. I paid for the `pro` subscription on [LabEx.io](https://labex.io/) to brush up and build a deeper, working knowledge of DevOps. The learning is structured well. Some of it gets repetitive - `cd` and `mkdir` drilled over and over - but the repetition is doing its job of reinforcing the basics.

Alongside that, I'm working through data structures and algorithms on [Hacker Rank](https://www.hackerrank.com/). The `Plus` subscription is enough to get guided practice from an AI tutor. Watching [MIT 6.006: Introduction to Algorithms](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-006-introduction-to-algorithms-spring-2020/) on its own wasn't enough - hands-on practice is what actually closes the gap.

The bigger move: I've applied to Georgia Tech's Online Master of Science in Computer Science (OMSCS) and I'm enrolling this month. Colleagues and friends who've been through it describe it as extremely tough and demanding.

I put this decision off for five years after finishing my Master of Science in Statistics at NUS back in 2021. Part of it was doubting whether a second Master's would add anything. Part of it was uncertainty - I still saw myself as a data scientist back then. Having spent time in both data science and software engineering roles since, I know which one I want to keep doing.

Knowing OMSCS will be tough doesn't change the calculation. It's the fastest way to build the foundations I'm missing and become a better engineer.

<hr>

Looking back, the path here wasn't direct - various data roles eventually led to software engineering, and along the way I picked up most of what I know informally. Enrolling in OMSCS is the decision to formalize that. The next couple of years will be hard. It'll also be the good kind of hard.
